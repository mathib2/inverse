## Sensible thermal energy
For a single phase with approximately constant specific heat capacity, sensible heat transfer is Q = m*c_p*(T_final - T_initial). Use compatible mass, energy and temperature units. With m = 2 kg, c_p = 4180 J/(kg K) and a temperature increase of 50 K, Q = 418000 J = 418 kJ. The specific heat is an explicit given value in this example, not a universal water-property prediction. State pressure, phase and the range over which the constant-property approximation is acceptable. This calculation neglects phase change, heat loss, container heat capacity and mechanical work. A reference formula does not supply an unknown material property. Distinguish an absolute temperature from a temperature difference and retain the sign of the transfer for the chosen system boundary.

## Thermodynamic energy balance
For a closed system, one common sign convention is change in internal energy equals heat added minus work done by the system. Declare the convention before using a balance. A steady-flow device may require enthalpy flow and kinetic or potential energy terms instead of the closed-system expression. Missing mass flow, state information or boundary conditions cannot be repaired by a fluent explanation. State which terms were neglected and why. Constant specific heat is an approximation rather than an automatic material property. An isolated system's total energy conservation does not imply that its temperature must stay constant. Phase equilibrium and real-fluid properties are outside the initial retrieval feature; they need separately validated property interfaces. Keep thermodynamic reference guidance separate from any claim that a numerical result has been independently checked.

## Hydrostatic fluid pressure
For an incompressible fluid at rest under uniform gravity, the pressure difference over vertical depth h is delta_p = rho*g*h. With density 1000 kg/m^3, gravity 9.81 m/s^2 and depth 2 m, the difference is 19620 Pa. This is a pressure difference; add a known surface absolute pressure only when absolute pressure is requested. Specify the positive depth direction. The model excludes flow-induced pressure losses and assumes density does not vary appreciably with depth. A negative height difference changes the sign. A pressure sensor may report gauge pressure, so preserve its reference rather than combining it with an unrelated absolute reading. Validate density, depth units and gravitational acceleration explicitly. A hydrostatic relation is not a pump curve or a pipe-friction correlation.

## Reynolds number and flow regime
For a circular pipe, Reynolds number is Re = rho*v*D/mu, using mean speed, internal diameter, density and dynamic viscosity. It is dimensionless. The equivalent expression v*D/nu uses kinematic viscosity, not dynamic viscosity. For rho = 1000 kg/m^3, v = 0.1 m/s, D = 0.01 m and mu = 0.001 Pa s, Re = 1000. This supports a laminar-flow example for a straight circular pipe. Transition is sensitive to disturbances and geometry; a numerical threshold is not universal proof of a regime. Check that properties correspond to the operating temperature and phase. Do not mix millimetres with metres or centipoise with pascal seconds. Carry the regime assumption into any subsequent friction-factor calculation and report uncertainty near transition.

## Laminar pipe pressure drop
For fully developed laminar flow of a Newtonian incompressible fluid in a straight circular pipe, the Darcy friction factor is f_D = 64/Re. The Darcy-Weisbach pressure drop is delta_p = f_D*(L/D)*(rho*v^2/2). With Re = 1000, f_D = 0.064, L = 10 m, D = 0.01 m, rho = 1000 kg/m^3 and v = 0.1 m/s, the pressure drop is 320 Pa. State that the factor is Darcy, not Fanning; confusing them introduces a factor of four. This example neglects entrance effects, fittings, elevation change and compressibility. A turbulent correlation must not be used merely because a package offers one. Record the regime, geometry, property provenance and equation identity together. The result is a calculated loss under these assumptions, not a measured pressure.

## Fluid flow rate and property limits
For mean velocity in a circular pipe, volumetric flow is Q_v = v*pi*D^2/4. Mass flow is density times volumetric flow when density is appropriately defined. With v = 0.1 m/s and D = 0.01 m, the volumetric flow is approximately 0.000007853981634 m^3/s. Distinguish radius from diameter and internal from external diameter. A diameter error is squared in the area. State operating temperature and pressure when converting volume flow to mass flow or comparing gas-flow measurements.
