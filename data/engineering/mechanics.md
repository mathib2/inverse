## Newtonian force and acceleration
For constant mass in an inertial frame, the resultant force is F = m*a. Force is a vector; its sign follows the selected coordinate direction. Convert mass to kilograms and acceleration to metres per second squared before calculating newtons. A worked example with m = 12.5 kg and a = -9.81 m/s^2 gives F = -122.625 N. Weight uses the local gravitational acceleration; mass and weight are different quantities. State whether the requested quantity is weight, resultant force, or a reaction. A reference equation alone does not determine unknown acceleration or prove a reported numerical result. Record inputs, units, assumptions and the expression actually evaluated. Check the dimensions independently and preserve the sign when rounding. This note assumes classical mechanics and does not model variable-mass systems or relativistic motion.

## Cantilever beam bending stress
For a rectangular cantilever carrying a transverse end load, the fixed-end bending moment magnitude is M = F*L. The second moment of area about the bending axis is I = b*h^3/12, where h is the dimension in the bending direction. The outer-fibre distance is c = h/2 and the bending stress magnitude is sigma = M*c/I = 6*F*L/(b*h^2). With F = 1000 N, L = 0.2 m, b = 0.04 m and h = 0.02 m, the result is 75 MPa. Assume a slender, prismatic, linearly elastic beam, small deflection and an ideal fixed support. Identify tensile and compressive sides separately. This nominal stress excludes fillets, holes, local contact, fatigue and stress concentrations. It is not a declaration that a bracket is safe. A material allowable, load case and safety-factor policy are separate required inputs.

## Cantilever beam deflection
The transverse tip deflection magnitude under an end load is delta = F*L^3/(3*E*I). For a rectangular section use I = b*h^3/12 about the actual bending axis. With the stress example's dimensions and E = 200 GPa, the tip deflection is 0.0005 m, or 0.5 mm. Assume constant elastic modulus, small rotation, a slender beam, negligible shear deflection and an ideal fixed support. Large displacement, a short thick section or an imperfect joint requires a different model. Report the direction according to the load sign. Recomputing this formula verifies substitution only; it does not verify support stiffness or the appropriateness of a beam idealization. Preserve the geometry provenance and avoid confusing the second moment of area with a mass moment of inertia.

## Axial stress and strain
Average axial normal stress is sigma = F/A for a centred load on a uniform cross-section. In the linear elastic uniaxial regime, strain is epsilon = sigma/E. Stress has pressure dimensions and strain is dimensionless. For F = 1000 N and A = 0.0001 m^2, the nominal stress is 10 MPa. If E = 200 GPa, the strain is 0.00005. Specify the tensile-positive or compressive-positive convention. This calculation excludes buckling, eccentric loading, plasticity, thermal stress and local concentrations near holes or load introduction. A negative stress can be physically meaningful and must not be silently replaced by its magnitude. Record the chosen area and material property; neither can be inferred from the force alone. Compare with an allowable only when the allowable and relevant failure mode are explicitly supplied.

## Torque and rotational acceleration
The torque vector is r cross F. Its magnitude is r*F*sin(theta), where theta is the angle between the position vector and force. A perpendicular 100 N force at a 0.2 m arm gives 20 N m. State the rotation-axis direction and use radians in numerical trigonometric functions unless the interface explicitly converts degrees. For rotation about a fixed principal axis with constant mass moment of inertia, alpha = torque/I. A supplied I = 2 kg m^2 gives alpha = 10 rad/s^2 in this example. Do not substitute an area moment of inertia from a beam calculation. Torque and energy share base dimensions but describe different physical quantities. Record which quantity is requested and keep the axis and reference point with the evidence.

## Mechanical energy and power
Translational kinetic energy is K = m*v^2/2. For constant force along a straight displacement, work is W = F*s*cos(theta). Average power is work divided by elapsed time; instantaneous mechanical power is the dot product of force and velocity. A 100 N force aligned with a velocity of 2 m/s transfers 200 W. State whether friction, stored elastic energy, rotation or gravity is included in the system boundary. A squared speed removes direction from kinetic energy but work and power can be negative. Do not infer efficiency without both input and useful-output definitions. Check joules versus watts and seconds versus hours before calculation. Conservation applies to a defined system; a damped oscillator loses mechanical energy while transferring energy to its surroundings.

## Damped oscillator parameter identification
For free linear motion, m*x'' + c*x' + k*x = 0. In an underdamped system the poles are -alpha plus or minus i*omega_d. With known positive mass, c = 2*m*alpha and k = m*(alpha^2 + omega_d^2). For m = 1 kg, alpha = 1 per second and omega_d = sqrt(99) rad/s, the identified coefficients are c = 2 N s/m and k = 100 N/m. The damping ratio is c/(2*sqrt(k*m)) = 0.1. These equations assume free response, viscous linear damping and identifiable poles. Check residuals against measurements and noise rather than retrieving hidden generator truth. Without known mass, these poles identify coefficient ratios, not unique mass, damping and stiffness. Report that ambiguity instead of inventing a mass.

## Oscillator validation and uncertainty
Parameter identification requires checking more than a fitted curve. Keep an independent validation interval, preserve sample times and sensor units, and compare displacement and velocity residuals with their measurement uncertainty. For positive mass and stiffness with nonnegative viscous damping, unforced mechanical energy should not grow. A higher damping coefficient does not automatically explain every observed change; altered stiffness, forcing or sensor calibration can also matter. With coefficients changing from c = 2 to c = 2.8 N s/m, the relative increase is (2.8 - 2)/2 = 0.4, or 40 percent. That percentage is valid only when the coefficients are themselves supported. The synthetic dataset generator is not a parameter-estimation tool and its hidden truth is not acceptable evidence for a recovery benchmark.
